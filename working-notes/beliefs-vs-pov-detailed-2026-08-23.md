# Beliefs vs Our Point of View - The common advice our clients hear, and what we believe instead

This is the reference behind our content. Left column is the advice, belief or assumption a business owner has almost certainly heard, usually from an accountant, a bookkeeper, another owner, or their own head. Right column is our point of view, clearly contrarian to that advice, in plain English. Use this to write rants, carousels, newsletters, and emails, you don't need a finance background to work from it, that's the point.

<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;That&rsquo;s what I pay my accountant for.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>You&rsquo;ll hear this from accountants themselves, and from other owners who&rsquo;ve been trained to see finance as someone else&rsquo;s department.</p>
<ul>
<li>Accountants are the trusted expert in the room, so it feels safe to hand the whole topic over to them</li>
<li>Most owners have never been shown a different way to relate to their own numbers, so outsourcing entirely feels like the responsible choice</li>
<li>The relationship usually only touches base monthly, quarterly, or at tax time, which looks like coverage even when it isn&rsquo;t</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>You can outsource the accounting. You can&rsquo;t outsource the decisions.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>An accountant&rsquo;s job is accounting, tax and compliance, not sitting next to you the day you decide whether to hire someone, raise a price, or make a big purchase</li>
<li>Every one of those calls happens between accountant conversations, which means the owner is making it blind or delaying it until the next catch up</li>
<li>Financial capability isn&rsquo;t a nice to have skill for owners, it&rsquo;s what turns &ldquo;I think this is a good idea&rdquo; into &ldquo;I know this is a good idea, and here&rsquo;s why&rdquo;</li>
<li>The goal was never to replace the accountant. It&rsquo;s for the owner to own enough of the picture that they&rsquo;re not waiting on anyone to make a call about their own business</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;I&rsquo;ve got a great bookkeeper, my numbers are always up to date.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>Comes from the idea that if the numbers are accurate, the job is done.</p>
<ul>
<li>Bookkeeping genuinely matters, and getting it wrong causes real problems, so owners are right to value it</li>
<li>Because the reports look clean and current, it&rsquo;s easy to assume clarity comes bundled in with accuracy</li>
<li>Most owners have never been shown the difference between data entry and interpretation, so the two jobs blur together</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Accurate data and financial clarity are two completely different products.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>A bookkeeper&rsquo;s job is to make sure the numbers are right, that transactions are coded properly and the reports reconcile</li>
<li>Nothing in that job description includes telling you what the numbers mean, what&rsquo;s driving them, or what to do about them</li>
<li>An owner can have flawless books and still have zero idea why cash is tight or what their next move should be</li>
<li>Clarity comes from interpretation, not accuracy. You need both, but they don&rsquo;t come from the same skill set, or the same person</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;We&rsquo;re bringing in a CFO a few days a month to help us get on top of it.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>Comes from a genuine, well meaning growth stage, the business feels too big to wing it, but not big enough for a full time hire.</p>
<ul>
<li>It feels like levelling up, bringing in more senior expertise than the business has had before</li>
<li>It&rsquo;s a fast fix that doesn&rsquo;t require the owner to build a new skill themselves</li>
<li>The market sells this as the mature, professional next step for a growing business</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>That&rsquo;s not empowerment, it&rsquo;s dependency with a bigger price tag.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>A part time finance hire can add real value, especially for a specific project or a defined stretch of growth</li>
<li>But if the whole point is for someone else to hold the understanding of your business, you&rsquo;re just renting a smarter version of the same problem</li>
<li>The real test: when that person isn&rsquo;t available, does the owner understand their own business any better than the day they started paying for help?</li>
<li>We believe in building financial capability into the business and the owner permanently, not renting it in by the month</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;I&rsquo;ve got five accounts now, profit, tax, wages, it&rsquo;s all separated out.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>A popular, visual system that gets recommended constantly because it&rsquo;s simple to set up and feels tangible.</p>
<ul>
<li>It&rsquo;s concrete and satisfying, you can literally watch the money move and see the balances</li>
<li>It gives a feeling of control without requiring any forecasting or numeracy</li>
<li>It&rsquo;s genuinely useful as a discipline device, which is exactly why it gets recommended so often</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Bank accounts organise your cash. They don&rsquo;t forecast it.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Multiple accounts are a fine guardrail system, moving percentages into separate buckets on a schedule</li>
<li>What they can&rsquo;t do is tell you what&rsquo;s coming in, what&rsquo;s going out, or when the next tight spot actually hits</li>
<li>An owner can have a perfectly organised set of accounts and still get blindsided by a BAS bill or a slow month</li>
<li>A cash flow forecast answers the question buckets can&rsquo;t: what can I actually afford to decide today, three months out?</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;We just need to sell more and this all sorts itself out.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>One of the oldest pieces of advice in business, because it&rsquo;s rarely wrong on its own, it&rsquo;s just wildly incomplete.</p>
<ul>
<li>More revenue feels like unambiguous progress, the easiest number to point to and celebrate</li>
<li>Sales is usually the part of the business the owner is most confident and comfortable talking about</li>
<li>It avoids the harder, more uncomfortable conversation about margin, capacity and cost structure</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>More revenue doesn&rsquo;t automatically mean more profit.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Growth can quietly drag wages, overheads, complexity and stress up at the same rate as revenue, sometimes faster</li>
<li>A business can grow 30% in a year and end up in a worse cash position than when it started</li>
<li>Revenue growth is brilliant when the underlying model works, and dangerous when it doesn&rsquo;t, because it just makes a broken model bigger and harder to unwind</li>
<li>Before chasing the next sale, know what happens to profit and cash when that sale actually lands</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;Profit&rsquo;s tight, we need to tighten our belts.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>The default, almost reflexive answer whenever profit is down, because it feels like the responsible, disciplined move.</p>
<ul>
<li>Cutting costs is something an owner can do unilaterally, today, without needing anyone else&rsquo;s buy in</li>
<li>It feels proactive and disciplined, which is emotionally satisfying when profit is a source of stress</li>
<li>Expense line items are visible and concrete, unlike pricing or margin, which can feel abstract</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Small cuts don&rsquo;t fix big profit problems.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Cutting $20k out of overhead might feel productive, but it&rsquo;s often nowhere near the size of the real opportunity sitting in pricing, margin, utilisation or conversion</li>
<li>Profit improvement isn&rsquo;t a synonym for cost cutting, it&rsquo;s about finding the highest leverage move available in that specific business</li>
<li>The better question isn&rsquo;t &ldquo;where can I save money,&rdquo; it&rsquo;s &ldquo;which number, if I moved it, would make the biggest difference to profit?&rdquo;</li>
<li>Sometimes that answer is costs. Often it&rsquo;s somewhere completely different, and you&rsquo;ll never find it if cost cutting is the only lever you know how to pull</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;Apparently we should be sitting at 20% margin and we&rsquo;re only at 12%.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>Comes from benchmarking culture, industry reports, podcasts, and other owners comparing notes.</p>
<ul>
<li>Benchmarks feel objective and reassuring, an external number to measure yourself against</li>
<li>It&rsquo;s simpler to chase one universal target than to work out your own business&rsquo;s specific economics</li>
<li>Everyone loves a round number, and 20% sounds like a serious, credible target</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Says who? Your business isn&rsquo;t their business.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Different industries and different business models run on completely different economics, a services business and a product business will never share the same margin structure</li>
<li>Chasing somebody else&rsquo;s benchmark is a distraction from the only question that actually matters for you</li>
<li>The real work is understanding what drives your margin specifically, then finding the biggest lever available inside your own numbers</li>
<li>A target profit percentage means nothing without knowing whether it&rsquo;s actually achievable, or even the right target, for your model</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;We&rsquo;ve got $300k in the account, we&rsquo;re doing great.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>The most intuitive, gut level way anyone reads their own financial position, because it&rsquo;s the number sitting right in front of them.</p>
<ul>
<li>It&rsquo;s the single easiest number to check, available instantly from a phone</li>
<li>It feels concrete and undeniable, unlike a P&amp;L or forecast, which requires interpretation</li>
<li>Most owners have never been taught that a bank balance is a snapshot, not a story</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Bank balance isn&rsquo;t profit. It isn&rsquo;t available cash. It isn&rsquo;t financial health.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>That $300k might have $100k earmarked for the ATO, payroll due next week, a BAS bill landing, and a big annual expense next month</li>
<li>A healthy looking number today says nothing about the version of that account that shows up in six weeks</li>
<li>The only way to know if $300k is actually healthy is to see what it has to cover, which is exactly what a weekly cash flow forecast shows you</li>
<li>The panic and confusion owners feel around cash almost always comes from reading the balance like a scoreboard instead of forecasting it forward</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;We made good profit this year, so cash should be fine.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>A very reasonable sounding assumption, because on the surface, profit and cash feel like the same thing.</p>
<ul>
<li>Profit is the number most owners are trained to watch and report on, so it&rsquo;s assumed to be the whole story</li>
<li>It&rsquo;s genuinely counterintuitive that a business can be profitable and still run out of money</li>
<li>Most financial education stops at the P&amp;L and never properly explains the difference</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>You can run a profitable business that&rsquo;s permanently cash starved.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Tax, debt repayments, stock, receivables, capital purchases, owner distributions and plain timing differences all consume cash that profit never accounts for</li>
<li>Profit tells you whether the business model works. Cash flow tells you whether you can actually execute the plan</li>
<li>An owner needs both numbers, and needs to understand that they answer two completely different questions</li>
<li>&ldquo;We made a profit, so where&rsquo;s the cash?&rdquo; is one of the most common, and most avoidable, moments of panic in business</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;If something was seriously wrong, surely my accountant would have said something.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>A passive but understandable assumption, since the accountant is the person qualified to spot financial trouble.</p>
<ul>
<li>It outsources the anxiety of watching the numbers to someone the owner trusts and pays for exactly that expertise</li>
<li>Most accountant relationships are built around compliance deadlines, not ongoing monitoring, but that distinction is rarely made clear to the client</li>
<li>It feels reasonable because accountants genuinely do catch real problems, just usually after the fact</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Waiting to be told is the most expensive way to run a business.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>By the time a problem shows up in a set of historical financial statements, the owner could have been making the wrong call for months</li>
<li>Financial statements are backward looking by nature, built to report what happened, not to warn you in real time</li>
<li>The owner is the only person in the business who&rsquo;s actually inside every decision, in real time, as it happens</li>
<li>The businesses that stay ahead of trouble are the ones where the owner is watching their own leading indicators, not waiting for a lagging report</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;Let&rsquo;s see how we went last month.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>How most owners were taught to relate to their numbers, check in once a month, see how you did.</p>
<ul>
<li>It mirrors how sport and performance are usually measured, after the fact, as a result</li>
<li>It requires no active decision making in the moment, just observation</li>
<li>It feels like doing the responsible thing without demanding much time or skill</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>A scoreboard tells you the score after the game&rsquo;s over. We want a control panel.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Knowing you made 8% profit last quarter doesn&rsquo;t tell you what to do differently at 10am tomorrow</li>
<li>Your numbers are made up of real, adjustable levers: price, volume, margin, team, growth spend, overheads, capacity</li>
<li>Understood properly, those levers let you decide the profit you want, compare it to the profit you&rsquo;ve actually got, and see exactly where to act</li>
<li>Stop checking the score after the siren&rsquo;s gone. Get your hands on the controls while the game is still being played</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;I know what revenue, gross profit and net profit mean, I&rsquo;m not clueless with numbers.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>A confidence built from genuine financial literacy, which most successful owners actually do have.</p>
<ul>
<li>Most owners at this level are smart, capable, and have picked up real financial vocabulary over the years</li>
<li>Understanding terminology feels like the finish line, because that&rsquo;s usually where financial education stops</li>
<li>Nobody ever draws the owner&rsquo;s attention to the gap between knowing the words and using them</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Knowing your numbers and using your numbers are two different skills.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Knowing what revenue, gross profit and net profit mean is not the same as being financially capable</li>
<li>The real test isn&rsquo;t whether you can define the terms, it&rsquo;s whether the numbers are actually shaping your pricing, hiring, marketing, capacity and growth decisions</li>
<li>If the numbers aren&rsquo;t influencing this week&rsquo;s decisions, you&rsquo;re reading them, not using them</li>
<li>Our clients aren&rsquo;t short on financial literacy. Their gap is financial decision making capability, and that&rsquo;s a completely different thing to teach and to learn</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;We do our budget every January, it&rsquo;s part of our planning process.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>A long standing, formal business ritual that feels like proper financial management.</p>
<ul>
<li>It feels thorough and professional, a document with a full year mapped out in detail</li>
<li>It&rsquo;s a one off task, which fits neatly into a busy owner&rsquo;s calendar once and then it&rsquo;s done</li>
<li>Nobody warned them that the value of a budget decays the moment reality starts to differ from it, which is almost immediately</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Don&rsquo;t budget where you think you&rsquo;ll land. Forecast where you&rsquo;re actually going.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reality changes three weeks into the year, and the annual budget quietly goes into a drawer</li>
<li>It gets dragged out occasionally, mostly to check whether you&rsquo;re ahead or behind a plan that&rsquo;s already stale</li>
<li>A forecast is a living document, updated as reality changes, not filed away and revisited once a quarter</li>
<li>The value isn&rsquo;t in predicting the future perfectly, it&rsquo;s in constantly comparing where you&rsquo;re headed against where you need to be, and adjusting in real time</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;We should probably be tracking more KPIs, revenue, leads, conversion, CAC, all of it.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>Comes from dashboard culture and the assumption that more visibility automatically means more control.</p>
<ul>
<li>More metrics feels like more rigour, more professionalism, more grown up business management</li>
<li>Software makes it incredibly easy to add another chart or number to a dashboard</li>
<li>Nobody ever explicitly tells the owner that more data has a cost too, in attention and confusion</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>More data doesn&rsquo;t create more clarity. Better interpretation does.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Revenue, leads, conversion, CAC, LTV, gross margin, EBITDA, utilisation, labour percentage, ROAS. Now there are 37 numbers on a dashboard and still no idea what to do on Monday morning</li>
<li>Financial clarity first isn&rsquo;t about volume of information, it&rsquo;s about knowing which number matters most right now</li>
<li>The real questions are simple: which number matters most right now, what is it telling us, and what are we going to do about it</li>
<li>80% of the value comes from focusing on the two or three numbers that actually drive this business, not from adding a 38th metric to the pile</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

<tr>
<td>
<p><em>&ldquo;Profit&rsquo;s just whatever&rsquo;s left over after everything else is paid.&rdquo;</em></p>
<p>The default, almost accidental way most owners think about profit, because it&rsquo;s literally how the maths works on a P&amp;L.</p>
<ul>
<li>It&rsquo;s mathematically true, revenue minus expenses does equal profit, so it feels like a fact rather than a mindset</li>
<li>It requires no active planning, you just wait and see what&rsquo;s left at the end of the month</li>
<li>Nobody ever shows owners an alternative, more intentional way to think about the number</li>
</ul>
</td>
<td>
<p><strong>Profit isn&rsquo;t leftover. Profit is designed.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Treating profit as whatever&rsquo;s left makes it something you discover after every decision has already been made, instead of something you plan for before you make them</li>
<li>Decide the profit the business actually needs to produce, then work backwards to find the levers that create it</li>
<li>Understand those levers, price, margin, cost structure, volume, then build towards the number on purpose</li>
<li>&ldquo;What was our profit?&rdquo; is the lowest value question you can ask about your business. &ldquo;What profit do we need, and what has to change to get there?&rdquo; is the one that actually moves something</li>
</ul>
</td>
</tr>

## How To Use This

- Every row lands on the same place: your numbers shouldn&rsquo;t just tell you how the business performed, they should tell you your next right move. That&rsquo;s the thread to keep coming back to across all fifteen.
- The left column explains why the ICP has almost certainly heard this and believed it, not because they&rsquo;re unsophisticated, but because it's genuinely reasonable-sounding advice that just leaves something important out.
- The right column is written the way I&rsquo;d actually say it to a client, not as accounting theory. Lift lines straight out of it for hooks, captions, and email opens.
- Signature phrases to weave in naturally where they fit: 80% is good enough, insights and actions not reporting and over analysis, focus on what matters, financial clarity first.
- Lead headlines with Profit or Cash Flow, not Clarity or Understanding, when you turn any of these into a title.
