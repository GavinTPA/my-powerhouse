# The Profit and Cash Flow Bricks
### Common advice we're here to throw a brick through

**What this is:** the beliefs, assumptions and "helpful" advice floating around out there that keep business owners dependent, confused or looking backwards instead of forwards. This is the ground I want to own. Every one of these is something a business owner has been told, by an accountant, a bookkeeper, a business coach, LinkedIn, or their own head, that sounds reasonable and quietly keeps them stuck.

None of this is "that advice is terrible, ignore it." Most of it is half right. The problem is what it leaves out. My job is to point at the gap and tell people what to do instead.

**How to use this doc:** each brick below is a rant, a carousel, a newsletter, an email subject line or a reel waiting to happen. Pick one, pick a fight with it, and land on what we actually believe. Signature phrases to weave in where they fit: *80% is good enough. Insights and actions, not reporting and over-analysis. Focus on what matters. Financial clarity first.*

Two items marked **(added)** aren't from the original brainstorm. I've pulled them straight out of our own methodology (Owe and Own, the Readiness Moment) because they're some of the best "mayor of the town" territory we've got. Cut them if they don't fit the batch.

---

## THE BIGGER PATTERN

Every brick below falls into one of five buckets. Worth keeping visible for the marketing team because it means we're not repeating ourselves, we're hitting the same target from five different angles.

| Bucket | What people believe | What we believe instead |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Outsource it** | Someone else can understand the numbers for me | You can outsource the accounting. You cannot outsource the decisions. |
| **2. Simplify it to one rule** | Sell more / cut costs / hit this margin / open five bank accounts | There's no universal answer. Your numbers tell you your next move, not somebody else's. |
| **3. Look backwards** | The numbers tell me what happened | The numbers should tell you what to do next |
| **4. Collect more information** | I need another dashboard, another KPI, another report | More data doesn't create clarity. Better interpretation does |
| **5. Mistake knowledge for capability** | I understand a P&L, so I'm good with numbers | Knowing your numbers and using your numbers are two different skills |

Our clients aren't short on financial literacy. Most of them already know what revenue and profit mean. Their problem is financial decision-making capability, knowing what to actually do about what the numbers are telling them. That's the whole business, and it's the thread running through every brick below.

## THE FLAGSHIP BRICKS
*The six with the most fight in them. Full treatment.*

### 🧱🧱🧱 1. Own Your Profit, Don't Rent It

**THE ADVICE:** Get yourself a great accountant, or better, hand the steering wheel to an outside finance hire. Let the numbers person understand the numbers and tell you what to do.

**THE FLAW:** That's financial dependence dressed up as financial support. A client of mine had outside finance help running his numbers for two years and still couldn't tell me why a profitable business had no cash in it. Worse, every time a decision came up, he had to wait for someone else to be available before he could act on his own business.

**THE BRICK:** Someone else can own the finance function. They cannot own your numbers for you. If you own the business, you need to own enough of the numbers to make a decision without waiting for permission.

**PROOF:** That same client came in with debt, tight cash flow, and no forecast, just "numbers for days." Once we simplified it down, he could finally see where the money went, why cash was tight, how much profit he actually needed, and what had to change to produce it.

**HOW TO USE IT:** Business owners have been told for years they'll "never really understand it." Say that out loud and challenge it directly. Dependence is convenient when you're the one selling the expertise someone depends on. A finance hire who makes themselves indispensable by keeping you confused hasn't helped you, they've made themselves your interpreter. A $3M business owner shouldn't need to book a meeting to understand what their own business is telling them.

---

### 🧱🧱🧱 2. Your Numbers Are a Control Panel, Not a Report Card

**THE ADVICE:** Your P&L tells you how the business performed last month, quarter or year. Read it, understand the result, move on.

**THE FLAW:** By the time you read it, the game's already been played. Knowing you made 8% profit last quarter doesn't tell you what to do at 10am tomorrow. You can get very good at reading financial statements and still be flying blind every time you make a decision.

**THE BRICK:** Your numbers are a control panel: price, volume, margin, team, growth spend, overheads, capacity. Understand those levers and you can decide the profit you want, compare it to the profit you've got, and see exactly where to act. That's the whole idea behind the Six Strategic Questions, two from cash flow, two from P&L, two from the balance sheet, run on repeat.

**PROOF:** This is exactly how a client went from "I made a profit, so where the hell is the cash?" to seeing precisely where the money was flowing and what had to change next.

**HOW TO USE IT:** Push harder than "use your numbers." Plenty of owners will hear that and think they already do, they check Xero once a month, gold star. The real challenge: if your numbers only tell you what happened, you're barely using them. The value is in the decisions hiding inside them. Stop staring at the scoreboard after the siren's gone. Get your hands on the controls.

---

### 🧱🧱 3. Small Cuts Don't Fix Big Profit Problems

**THE ADVICE:** Profit's tight? Cut expenses. Review the subscriptions. Trim the overhead. Save money wherever you can.

**THE FLAW:** You can pour enormous energy into squeezing tiny costs while ignoring the giant lever sitting right next to it. There's a ceiling on what trimming overhead can ever produce. You feel financially responsible while making a marginal dent in the real opportunity.

**THE BRICK:** Don't ask "where can I save money?" Ask "which number gives me the biggest return on my attention?" Your numbers should rank the problem for you, that's what the Profit Formula does: compare where you are to where you want to be and show you the one move that matters most.

**PROOF:** A jewellery client was obsessing over overheads sitting at around 20% of costs, while cost of sales sat at 50 to 60%. Once attention shifted to pricing, job economics, wastage, markups and upsells, the real profit opportunity was obvious, and the results followed.

**HOW TO USE IT:** Business owners love fixing small, visible expenses. Cancel the software, question the phone bill, save $400, feel productive. Meanwhile there's tens of thousands sitting in pricing or margin, untouched. A business owner with ten possible improvements doesn't need another to-do list, they need to know which one moves the most money. Stop working hard on small numbers.

---

### 🧱🧱🧱 4. Cash in the Bank Isn't Cash Flow You Can Trust

**THE ADVICE:** There's $300k in the bank. The business is doing well.

**THE FLAW:** Except $100k of that belongs to the ATO, payroll's due next week, the BAS is coming, suppliers haven't been paid, and there's a big annual bill next month. Bank balance is not profit. Bank balance is not available cash. Bank balance is not financial health. It's just a number sitting in an account at a moment in time.

**THE BRICK:** You need to see your next low point before you're in it, not after. That's the entire point of a weekly cash flow forecast: it tells you what's coming in, what's going out, when the tight spots hit, and what you can actually afford to decide today without getting surprised in six weeks.

**PROOF:** Every single client who's ever said "I don't understand why the money isn't there" has been reading their bank balance like a scoreboard instead of forecasting forward. The moment they can see three months out, the panic disappears, because they can see the low point coming and plan around it instead of reacting to it.

**HOW TO USE IT:** This is one of the most dangerous assumptions in business and it's an easy one to dramatise: read out a real bank balance, then read out what it actually has to cover. The gap is the whole story. A healthy-looking number today tells you nothing about the version of your bank account that shows up in six weeks.

---

### 🧱🧱🧱 5. Profit Isn't Leftover. Profit Is Designed.

**THE ADVICE:** Revenue minus expenses equals whatever's left. That's your profit.

**THE FLAW:** That makes profit something you discover at the end of the month, after every decision's already been made. You find out what you got instead of deciding what you wanted.

**THE BRICK:** Profit should be designed, not discovered. Decide the profit the business needs to produce. Understand the levers that create it. Then build towards that number on purpose. That's literally what the Profit Calculator does: it tells you the next right move for profitability, not last month's result.

**PROOF:** Every client who walks in treating profit as "whatever's left" is running the business backwards. The ones who set the target first and work back to the decision are the ones who stop being surprised by their own numbers.

**HOW TO USE IT:** This one reframes the entire conversation from reporting to engineering. "What was our profit?" is the lowest-value question you can ask about your own business. "What profit do we need, and what has to change to get there?" is the one that actually moves anything.

---

### 🧱🧱 6. You Were Never Taught to Use Your Numbers *(added)*

**THE ADVICE:** Some people are just "good with numbers" and some people aren't. If you're not a numbers person, get someone else to handle that side.

**THE FLAW:** This is the belief sitting underneath almost every other one on this list. It's not true, and it's the single biggest thing keeping capable business owners financially dependent. Nobody's born knowing how to read a business's cash flow. It's a skill, the same way pricing, hiring and sales are skills. It just never gets taught, so owners assume they lack an aptitude they were simply never given.

**THE BRICK:** You don't need to be good with numbers. You need a simple framework that shows you what matters. Once an owner has that, the fear disappears, because it was never about talent, it was about nobody ever handing them the method.

**PROOF:** This is the exact moment every client hits, the Readiness Moment, where they stop assuming the numbers are someone else's territory and start realising it was always learnable.

**HOW TO USE IT:** This is the most personal, most "mayor of the town" brick on the list. It's not really about advice out there, it's about a lie business owners have been quietly telling themselves for years. Worth its own rant, its own email, its own carousel, on its own.

## THE REST OF THE BRICKS
*Same fight, shorter form. Organised by bucket.*

### Bucket 1: Outsource It

**"A good bookkeeper is all you need."**
Accurate bookkeeping matters. Accurate data doesn't automatically create clarity. A bookkeeper makes sure the numbers are right. That's not the same as telling you what they mean or what to do next.
*Use it as:* a "two different jobs" carousel, bookkeeping vs interpretation.

**"Your accountant will tell you if something's wrong."**
That's a dangerous, passive way to run a business. By the time a problem shows up in historical financial statements, you could've been making the wrong call for six months. You shouldn't be waiting for someone else to tell you what's happening inside your own business.
*Use it as:* a rant on why "no news from the accountant" isn't the same as "everything's fine."

**"Pay for some advisory sessions and they'll tell you what to do."**
Periodic advisory has real value. Talking to someone once a month or once a quarter isn't the same as having the capability to confidently make financial decisions every week. We're not anti-accountant. We're anti-dependency.
*Use it as:* a line for an email about the difference between advice and capability.

### Bucket 2: Simplify It to One Rule

**"Don't worry about the numbers, just focus on sales."**
More revenue doesn't automatically mean more profit. You can grow revenue while growing wages, overheads, complexity and stress right alongside it. Growth can hide a broken business model for a surprisingly long time.
*Use it as:* the "we just need another $500k" myth, paired with a client example where growth made the problem worse, not better.

**"If you want more profit, cut costs."**
Sometimes that's exactly the move. But cutting $20k of expenses is often nowhere near as valuable as fixing pricing, utilisation, conversion, capacity or gross margin. Profit improvement isn't a synonym for cost cutting, it's finding the highest-leverage move available.
*Use it as:* companion piece to the "Small Cuts" flagship brick above.

**"A good business should make X% profit."**
Maybe. Maybe not. Different industries and business models run on completely different economics. Chasing somebody else's benchmark is a distraction from the only question that matters: what drives your margin, and which lever there is the biggest opportunity.
*Use it as:* a "stop comparing your margin to a stranger's business" post.

**"Set up multiple bank accounts and cash flow takes care of itself."**
Multiple accounts create useful guardrails. Moving money between buckets doesn't tell you what's coming in, what's going out, when the tight spots are, or what you can afford to decide today. Bank accounts organise your cash. A forecast helps you make decisions with it.
*Use it as:* a visual, buckets vs a forecast line graph.

### Bucket 3: Look Backwards

**"If you're profitable, cash will take care of itself."**
You can run a profitable business that's permanently cash-starved. Tax, debt repayments, inventory, receivables, capex, distributions and timing all eat cash that profit never accounts for. Profit tells you the model works. Cash flow tells you whether you can execute the plan. You need both.
*Use it as:* pairs naturally with the "Cash in the Bank" flagship above.

**"Just make a budget at the start of the year."**
Reality changes three weeks later. The budget goes in a drawer and gets dragged out occasionally to check if you're ahead or behind. Don't budget where you think you'll land. Forecast where you're going, and keep updating the plan as reality changes.
*Use it as:* a "your budget died in February, here's what replaces it" post.

**"Financial management means logging into Xero and looking at reports."**
Log in. Run the P&L. Look at last month. Nod thoughtfully. Close Xero. That's not financial management. The value was never in the report itself, it's in the chain from numbers to insight to decision to action to result. The report is only the starting point.
*Use it as:* a short, dry, funny reel acting out the ritual, then cutting to what should happen instead.

### Bucket 4: Collect More Information

**"You need to track more KPIs."**
Revenue, leads, conversion, CAC, LTV, gross margin, EBITDA, utilisation, labour %, ROAS. Now the owner's staring at 37 numbers and still doesn't know what to do on Monday morning. More data doesn't create more clarity. The real questions are: which number matters most right now, what's it telling us, and what are we doing about it.
*Use it as:* a "dashboard graveyard" visual, 37 tiles down to the 6 that matter.

### Bucket 5: Mistake Knowledge for Capability

**"I know how to read a P&L, so I know my numbers."**
Knowing what revenue, gross profit and net profit mean isn't the same as being financially capable. There's a real gap between knowing your numbers and using your numbers. If the numbers aren't shaping your pricing, hiring, marketing, capacity or growth decisions, you're not really using them.
*Use it as:* the "knowing vs using" split test, good as a simple two-column graphic.

**"The balance sheet is for the bank, not for me." (added)**
Most owners have never really looked at their own balance sheet. They think of it as something you produce for a lender or an accountant, not something that changes how you run the business. In plain English, it's just what you owe and what you own. Ignore it and you're making decisions on half the picture, your P&L tells you if the model works, your balance sheet tells you what you're actually carrying while you run it.
*Use it as:* an "Owe and Own" explainer, this term is genuinely ownable, worth building a recurring content series around it.
