How to use this

We looked at every sales call from October (plus one in November) and asked one question: what was different about the people who said yes? This doc gives you the answer so the ads attract more of the people who buy and fewer of the people who stall.

  • Pages 2 to 3: who the four buyers are and the ten things they have in common.
  • Pages 4 to 5: the nine who didn't buy, and what made them different.
  • Page 6: the sharpened customer profile and what it means for ads, landing page and booking form.

The one-line version

The people who buy are hurting now, decide alone, and want to understand their own numbers. Revenue size mattered less than that. Two of the four buyers sat outside our $1M to $5M band and still closed easily.

What the four buyers have in common

1They carry the money in their head, alone

Each one is the sole financial decision maker. Partners, siblings and managers defer to them, so nobody said "I need to talk to my partner". Adam: "at the end of the day, I'm the one that steers the ship." Rachel: "quite lonely."

2The tax bill is the wound

Three of four carry ATO debt, and the fourth says BAS drains the account. It was the most emotional moment in every call. Adam: "We can't afford to pay the BAS... that's the keeping awake at night." Suzie: "a tax man took it all."

3Revenue is fine, profit has gone missing

All four sit at or near break-even on a decent top line. Their words match our ad language almost exactly. Suzie: "how am I profitable, but I have no money in the bank?" Xavier: "Mr Taxman says I've made that much profit... it's not there."

4They pay an accountant and have hit the ceiling of that

None want to fire the accountant. They've worked out it looks backwards. The gap is "someone to tell me what to do next". Rachel: with the accountant "you sort of feel like a bit of a dum-dum."

5People-heavy service businesses

Wages are the biggest cost in all four. Adam and Suzie describe the same pattern: four-week months look profitable, five-week months lose money.

6Brilliant at their craft, never shown the numbers

Adam: "I never got any management schooling." Xavier: "I'm not a numbers person." Rachel: "I don't do technology very well."

7A personal pressure point tipped them over

Two said "enough is enough" almost word for word. Suzie took the call from her dying father's nursing home. Adam's father had a stroke. Rachel: "it's definitely swallowed me whole."

8A concrete thing they want, and no map to get there

New trucks and a GM, a saleable business, SIL homes, a commercial shed. Not vague "growth".

9They already spend money on help

Price never came up as an objection. Cash timing did, which is why all four paid weekly and why the guarantee mattered.

10Warm before they arrived

All four had a setter conversation first. They arrived already believing they had the problem. The call confirmed fit, it didn't create the need.

What landed on the call

  • Gavin as a fellow owner, not just an accountant
  • The bars story (Xavier: "Wow." Suzie: "Can you do that for me?")
  • Proof from their industry
  • Coach, referee, scorekeeper analogy
  • Weekly payments plus the cash flow guarantee

Their questions (not objections)

  • "Will I be dealing with you, or a junior?"
  • "How do you actually implement this?"
  • "When do we start, I'm flat out until Christmas"
  • "As long as you feel I'm a correct person"

The pattern in one look

Mostly profitable and comfortable. Most share the decision with someone who wasn't on the call. Most already have something filling the gap (a forecast, CFO, coach, franchise support). Five of nine are in hospitality, leisure or accommodation. None of the buyers are.

Two things worth knowing

Deciding alone is necessary, not enough

Three non-buyers also decided alone (Alex, Clayton, Ramy). They stalled for other reasons: never heard the offer, didn't like the upfront lump, or had no money pain.

"Timing" was the excuse, not the cause

Buyers had awful timing too. Suzie was in a palliative ward, Adam was heading into peak season, Xavier was flat out to Christmas. They bought anyway. Xavier: "At the time it happens, it's never the right time." When the pain is high and one person decides, timing doesn't stop the sale.

Sales process gaps (for Gavin and Melinda)

  • Partner absent and no joint call booked (Phillip, Michael, Brad, Kaveh).
  • Price came late, 48 to 70 minutes in. Alex never got it.
  • Calls ran long (95 and 77 minutes). Long software walk-throughs got "Yeah" in reply.
  • Calls ended with a link in the Zoom chat and nothing in the calendar.
  • Raj came to cut tax and didn't know what Gavin does. That should be screened out before the call.

The sharpened customer profile

Buys now

  • One owner who holds the money alone
  • Break-even or thin profit
  • Live tax pain: ATO debt or a BAS shock
  • People-heavy service business
  • Only help is a backwards-looking accountant
  • Wants to understand it themselves
  • Has hit "enough is enough"

Stalls

  • Profitable and comfortable
  • Arrived cold
  • A partner shares the decision and isn't on the call
  • Already has a forecast, CFO, coach or franchise support
  • Wants it done for them
  • Mid-expansion or multi-entity
  • Came to cut tax

What this means for the ads

Our job is to bring in more people who look like the left-hand box and fewer who look like the right. Here's how to use the study.

1. Use their words, not ours

Hooks straight from the buyers' mouths: "How am I profitable, but I have no money in the bank?" "The tax man took it all." "Money comes in, it's gone." "We can't afford to pay the BAS." And the five-week pay month that wipes out the profit from the four-week months.

2. Lead with tax pain and missing profit

The ATO bill was the most emotional moment on every buyer call. Profit on paper, nothing in the bank, then the tax bill lands. That's the story to tell.

3. Show people-heavy service businesses

Clinics, trades, freight, NDIS, installers: businesses where wages are the biggest cost. Be careful leaning on hospitality creative. It gets clicks (Kaveh: "that's the ad that got me") but 5 of the 9 non-buyers came from hospitality, leisure or accommodation, and none of the buyers did.

4. Speak to the owner who wants to learn it

"Know your own numbers" and "stop waiting for the accountant" pull in learners. Anything that sounds like "we'll do it for you" pulls in the delegators who stall.

5. Screen on the landing page and booking form

Ask who makes the money decisions, and if it's shared, ask them to bring that person to the call. Make clear this is about understanding your numbers, not cutting your tax. Don't filter hard on revenue: pain predicted the close better than size did.

6. Warm them up before the call

All four buyers had a setter conversation first. A cold booking from an ad needs that warm-up, or the call has to create the need from scratch, and it doesn't stick.
